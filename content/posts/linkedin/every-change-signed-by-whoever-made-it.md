A single-vendor AI tool cannot tell you who changed your document. It only ever sees itself.

That sounds like a small gap. It isn't.

Take a document that matters — a PRD, a positioning brief, a set of meeting notes. It gets drafted in an AI chat. Pasted into a shared doc. Your co-founder opens it, feeds it to *their* assistant, asks for a tighten, pastes the result back.

Nothing dramatic happens. That's the problem.

Each pass changes a little. A qualifier drops. A number rounds. A decision that had a reason behind it survives as a sentence without the reason. Two weeks later the document reads fine, and nobody can say which parts were decided and which parts were interpolated.

The failure isn't that the work got lost. It's that it got quietly rewritten, by more hands than anyone can name.

Ask your tools who last changed that file. An assistant knows what it did. It cannot see your co-founder's edit in a different tool, or what the other assistant changed on Thursday. A ledger that records one of the actors is not a ledger. It's a diary.

Which is why persistence and attribution aren't the same capability, and why the second can't be bolted onto the first. Persistence is storage: keep the file between sessions. Attribution is a protocol: every writer identifies itself at the moment of writing. If the writers were never asked to sign, no amount of later processing recovers the signature.

The test is one question you should be able to answer in ten seconds about any file you care about: who last changed this, and what did they change?

If the answer requires you to remember, you have storage. If it's on the screen with a name on it, you have a record.
